# What Goes Wrong — Clause Types That Burn People

Research question: which recurring clause types actually cause documented harm to consumers,
freelancers, tenants, and small businesses, and how do they rank by how often they show up in
real complaints/evidence (FTC actions, lawsuits, consumer-protection data, forum/complaint
patterns)?

**Caveat on ranking**: Only two categories (arbitration clauses, auto-renewal/cancellation
traps) have hard, comparable statistics behind them. The rest are ranked by strength and
recency of documented evidence (regulatory actions, law-firm client-education patterns citing
real disputes, forum complaint clustering) rather than by a common frequency metric. Where the
evidence doesn't support a precise ranking, that is called out explicitly below.

## Ranked findings

### 1. Arbitration clauses / class-action waivers — strongest quantified evidence
**What happens**: Buries a "you can't sue us, and you can't join a class action" clause in
fine print. Consumers/employees are pushed into individual, confidential arbitration where the
economics make most small-dollar claims not worth pursuing, and win rates are low.
**Evidence**:
- 81 of the Fortune 100 use consumer arbitration agreements; 78 of those also include class
  action waivers. At least a majority (potentially two-thirds) of U.S. households are covered,
  and over 60% of retail e-commerce sales involve such agreements. As of 2018, an estimated
  826 million consumer arbitration agreements were active, but only ~411 arbitration claims/year
  were actually filed across six major financial product categories (2010–2012) — evidence the
  clause suppresses claims rather than resolving them. Consumers win relief in only about 9% of
  arbitration disputes. — [Center for Justice & Democracy fact sheet](https://centerjd.org/content/fact-sheet-forced-arbitration-clauses-and-class-actions-waivers-numbers)
- More than 60 million private-sector, non-union employees are subject to forced arbitration;
  nearly half of those also waive class actions. The clauses disproportionately bind low-wage
  workers (the majority of all workers earning under $13/hr). Employer use rose from 37.2%
  (2017) to 48.9% (2018) of companies. — [Impact Fund, "Toothless Rights"](https://www.impactfund.org/legal-practitioner-blog/toothless-rights)
- Consumer Reports frames this as a systemic consumer-protection issue, not an isolated one. — [Consumer Reports, "Forced Arbitration: A Clause for Concern"](https://www.consumerreports.org/mandatory-binding-arbitration/forced-arbitration-clause-for-concern/)
**Contract types**: Employment agreements, consumer ToS (financial products, retail/e-commerce), gig work.

### 2. Auto-renewal / hard-to-cancel subscription clauses — strongest quantified consumer-harm evidence
**What happens**: Easy sign-up, deliberately obstructed cancellation (login walls, in-person-only
cancellation, staff trained to refuse cancellation requests, rebilling under new account numbers
after a customer thinks they cancelled).
**Evidence**:
- FTC sued Fitness International/LA Fitness (also Esporta Fitness, City Sports Club) alleging
  members had to retrieve login credentials, print a form, and mail it or appear in person
  9am–5pm at gyms open 19 hours/day; staff were allegedly trained to deny phone/email
  cancellation requests; customers were rebilled under new account numbers after attempting to
  cancel. FTC said "tens of thousands" of customers reported cancellation difficulty. — [Gizmodo, "FTC Sues Gym Chains for Making It Hard to Cancel Memberships"](https://gizmodo.com/cancel-gym-membership-la-fitness-ftc-2000645883)
- NY AG separately settled with Aneva Gym (May 2025) and Equinox Group (Aug 2025) over
  hard-to-cancel memberships. — [Davis Wright Tremaine, "Stay ADvised"](https://www.dwt.com/insights/2025/11/stay-advised-brand-protection-november-10)
- Nationally: ~42% of Americans have complained about difficult cancellation processes; 59% of
  consumers report experiencing deceptive free-trial-to-paid conversion; BBB complaints about
  free trials doubled 2015–2017; average loss per incident was $186. — [National Consumers League, "Enough is enough!"](https://nclnet.org/ftc_autorenew/)
**Contract types**: Consumer subscription ToS — gyms, streaming, SaaS free trials, wellness services.

### 3. Personal guarantees (commercial leases / small-business contracts) — well-documented but not quantified
**What happens**: A business owner signs a lease or vendor contract "as guarantor." If the
business fails or defaults, the landlord/creditor can sue the individual personally and go after
personal assets — not just the business entity's assets — once business assets are exhausted.
**Evidence**: Multiple law-firm client-education sources independently describe the same fact
pattern — landlord sues both the business and the guarantor, and pursues personal assets after
business assets run out — as a routine, recurring outcome of small-business leasing, with
defenses turning on whether the guarantee was "full" vs. "good guy"/limited and whether the
landlord mitigated damages. — [FindLaw, "What Is a Personal Guarantee Commercial Lease (and How to Get out of One)"](https://www.findlaw.com/legalblogs/small-business/what-is-a-personal-guarantee-commercial-lease-and-how-to-get-out-of-one/); [Southron Firm, "Sued on a Personal Guarantee in Florida"](https://www.southronfirm.com/blog/2026/sued-personal-guarantee-florida/)
**Contract types**: Commercial leases, vendor/supplier credit agreements for small businesses.
**Note on frequency**: No aggregate complaint count found; evidence is pattern-consistency across
independent legal-practice sources describing actual lawsuits, not a statistic.

### 4. Scope creep / unlimited revisions — high forum-complaint density, not independently quantified
**What happens**: Vague scope language lets a client keep demanding "revisions" indefinitely,
or withholds final payment until unpaid extra work is done.
**Evidence**: Analysis of r/freelance and r/Upwork identifies scope creep and revision disputes
as two of the four most recurring complaint categories (alongside late payment and client
disappearance). Direct quoted example: a client "approved the design, [freelancer] sent the
final files, then they claimed they wanted revisions not covered in our agreement and refused to
pay the final 50% until [the freelancer] made unlimited changes." The consistent fix cited across
threads is a defined number of included revision rounds with anything beyond billed separately. — [PainPointMap, "What Reddit Reveals About Freelance Payment Problems"](https://www.painpointmap.com/blog/freelance-payment-problems-reddit)
**Contract types**: Freelance/independent-contractor agreements (design, writing, dev work).

### 5. Non-compete clauses — active regulatory/enforcement evidence, scope narrowing
**What happens**: Restricts a worker's ability to take a new job or start a competing business,
sometimes imposed on low-wage or non-executive workers who have little bargaining power.
**Evidence**: FTC's 2024 rule attempting to ban nearly all employer-worker non-competes was
vacated after litigation (Sept 2025), but the FTC has continued case-by-case enforcement — e.g.,
a September 2025 complaint against Gateway Services Inc. (pet cremation) and its subsidiary over
non-competes imposed on employees, with continued scrutiny in staffing and healthcare. — [FTC press releases](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-cracks-down-companies-impose-harmful-noncompete-restrictions-thousands-workers); [American Staffing Association, "Beyond the Ban"](https://americanstaffing.net/posts/2026/01/07/beyond-the-ban/)
**Contract types**: Employment agreements (notably lower-wage/non-executive workers, staffing, healthcare).
**Note**: Regulatory attention is real, but with the federal rule vacated, harm now shows up
case-by-case (individual FTC actions, state-law fights) rather than as a single national
statistic.

### 6. IP assignment ambiguity (freelance/gig work) — documented dispute pattern, not quantified
**What happens**: Clients assume they own everything a freelancer produces; under default law,
freelancers actually own what they create unless a valid assignment (not just a "work for hire"
label) transfers it — and "work for hire" doesn't legally apply to many categories of creative
work (logos, websites, illustrations, marketing copy). Signing a legally shaky "work for hire"
clause still triggers real disputes and litigation/arbitration.
**Evidence**: Independent legal-practice sources (Aaron Hall; PatentPC) converge on the same
pattern — ambiguous contract terms and differing interpretations of work-for-hire status are a
frequent driver of ownership disputes that end up in litigation or arbitration. — [Aaron Hall, "IP Ownership Clauses for Gig Workers & Freelancers"](https://aaronhall.com/ip-ownership-clauses-for-gig-workers-freelancers/); [PatentPC, "How to Prevent IP Ownership Disputes with Contractors and Freelancers"](https://patentpc.com/blog/how-to-prevent-ip-ownership-disputes-with-contractors-and-freelancers)
**Contract types**: Freelance/independent contractor agreements (design, dev, creative work).

### 7. Indemnification clauses — described as high-severity but low-visibility, not quantified
**What happens**: A broadly worded indemnification clause can make a freelancer or small
business responsible for funding another party's legal defense and damages before fault is even
determined — and many liability insurance policies exclude contractual indemnification, so the
exposure is often uninsured.
**Evidence**: Multiple law-firm client-education articles describe indemnification as a "hidden
danger" that can financially sink a small business, citing the concrete example of a content
writer who publishes an article and becomes financially responsible for a defamation or
plagiarism lawsuit brought against the client/publication. — [MHS Law, "The Hidden Danger in Your Contracts: Indemnification Clauses That Can Sink a Small Business"](https://mhslaw.com/the-hidden-danger-in-your-contracts-indemnification-clauses-that-can-sink-a-small-business/); [Indy, "What Is an Indemnification Clause in a Freelance Contract?"](https://weareindy.com/blog/what-is-an-indemnification-clause-in-a-freelance-contract)
**Contract types**: Freelance contracts, small-business vendor/service agreements.
**Note**: This is consistently flagged as *high-severity when it hits*, but the sourcing found is
educational/anecdotal (law firms describing the mechanism and a representative scenario), not a
complaint count.

### 8. Liability caps — courts flag this as contested, evidence is doctrinal rather than complaint-based
**What happens**: Vendor caps its financial exposure (commonly to 12 months of fees paid),
leaving the customer under-compensated if the vendor's failure (e.g., a data breach or outage)
causes damages far exceeding that cap.
**Evidence**: Legal-practice sources note that without a cap, a small contract dispute could
produce a disproportionate judgment, but also note courts respond with "skepticism and downright
hostility" toward liability caps asserted against a small business or consumer, especially absent
a data-breach carve-out. — [Hyperstart/CloudNuro-style guidance, "Indemnity and Liability Caps in SaaS"](https://www.cloudnuro.ai/blog/saas-liability-cap)
**Contract types**: SaaS/vendor agreements.
**Note**: No specific lawsuit or complaint example was found in this search pass — evidence here
is doctrinal/advisory rather than a documented real-world dispute.

### 9. Fee escalators / rent escalation clauses — documented as a surprise-cost pattern, not quantified
**What happens**: Rent tied to CPI, fixed annual percentage increases, or pass-through CAM
(common-area-maintenance) charges can push a tenant's costs up unpredictably, sometimes doubling
costs over a lease term; disputes center on how the increase was calculated.
**Evidence**: Legal-practice sources describe business owners being "surprised when routine
operating costs or CPI increases push their payments far higher than expected," and define an
"escalation claim" as a recognized category of lease dispute over calculation/interpretation of
the clause. — [JDE Law, "The Escalation Clause | Hidden Contract Increases Explained"](https://www.jdelaw.nyc/blog-the-escalation-clause/); [Best Lawyers, "State of Rent Escalation Clauses"](https://www.bestlawyers.com/article/rent-escalation-clauses-leasing-agreement/5129)
**Contract types**: Commercial leases.

### 10. Early termination penalties (commercial leases) — documented mechanism, not quantified
**What happens**: Breaking a lease early can trigger liability for the remainder of rent owed,
liquidated damages, and sometimes punitive damages under state law; disputes often turn on
whether the landlord met its duty to mitigate by re-leasing the space.
**Evidence**: Legal-practice sources describe this as a standard, recurring landlord remedy
(commonly 3–6 months of rent plus unamortized lease-up costs when negotiated), with disputes
arising when landlords fail to promptly re-lease. — [UpCounsel, "Understanding the Commercial Lease Early Termination Clause"](https://www.upcounsel.com/commercial-lease-early-termination-clause); [Spacebase, "Understanding the Legal Risks of Early Commercial Lease Termination"](https://spacebaseapp.com/blog/understanding-the-legal-risks-of-early-commercial-lease-termination/)
**Contract types**: Commercial leases.

### 11. Unilateral termination clauses (SaaS/ToS) — plausible risk, weakest evidence found
**What happens**: A vendor can suspend or terminate an account "in its sole discretion" for an
alleged violation, with the customer having no equivalent right and sometimes no cure period.
**Evidence**: Legal-practice sources describe this as a common SaaS contract feature and state
that "vague or poorly drafted termination clauses often lead to arbitration or lawsuits," but no
specific named lawsuit, complaint statistic, or forum thread pattern was found in this search
pass to confirm real-world frequency. — [Sirion, "Understanding Unilateral Termination of Contract"](https://www.sirion.ai/library/contract-obligations/unilateral-termination-of-contract/?libid=31)
**Contract types**: SaaS terms of service.
**This is the weakest-evidenced category on the list** — flagged here as plausible/commonly
discussed in legal literature, not confirmed by a documented complaint or case.

### 12. Data/privacy rights grabs (ToS content licenses, data collection) — one landmark case, general litigation trend
**What happens**: ToS grants a platform broad, sometimes perpetual rights to user content and
data; users "consent" to tracking of browsing habits, location, or messages via fine print they
didn't meaningfully read.
**Evidence**: The Cambridge Analytica case (Facebook sharing ~87 million users' data without
adequate consent) is the widely cited landmark example. More broadly, there is a rising litigation
trend of consumers using a company's own privacy notice/ToS/advertising language as the basis for
breach-of-contract and breach-of-warranty claims. — [IAPP, "US Data Privacy Litigation: Breach of contract and warranties litigation"](https://iapp.org/resources/article/us-litigation-series-breach-contract-warranties); [TALG, "The Fine Print: Social Media Terms of Service"](https://talglaw.com/the-fine-print-social-media-terms-of-service/)
**Contract types**: Consumer/social-media ToS.

## What I could not find

- **No single-source, apples-to-apples frequency ranking across all 12 clause types.** Only
  arbitration clauses and auto-renewal/cancellation clauses have real quantitative complaint or
  prevalence data behind them (FTC actions with specific complaint counts, BBB complaint trend
  data, arbitration filing/win-rate statistics). Everything below those two is ranked by
  strength/consistency of qualitative evidence (independent legal-practice sources converging on
  the same fact pattern, named lawsuits/enforcement actions, or forum complaint clustering), not
  by a comparable frequency number. Treat the ranking below #2 as impressionistic, not
  quantitative.
- **Unilateral termination clauses (SaaS)** had the weakest evidence of the twelve candidates —
  commonly discussed as a risk in legal literature, but no specific lawsuit, FTC action, or forum
  thread was surfaced in this search pass to confirm it burns people in practice as often as
  claimed. It is included because the underlying mechanism (sole-discretion termination, no cure
  period) is corroborated by contract-law sources, but flagged as unconfirmed by real complaint
  evidence.
- **Liability caps and early-termination penalties** are described in doctrinal/advisory terms
  (how courts tend to view them, how they're typically structured) rather than via a specific
  documented complaint or lawsuit found in this pass.
- **No FTC/state-AG complaint-volume breakdown by clause type** was found (e.g., a dataset
  showing "X% of consumer complaints cite arbitration clauses vs. Y% cite auto-renewal"). The
  FTC and NCL sources speak to auto-renewal/cancellation specifically, not a cross-clause
  comparison.
- **Personal guarantees, IP assignment, indemnification, and fee escalators** are all supported
  by multiple independent legal-practice sources describing the same real-world fact pattern
  (which is meaningful corroboration) but none of these come with an aggregate statistic or named
  court case discovered in this pass — evidence is closer to "consistently reported pattern" than
  "measured prevalence."
- Did not search litigation-database sites (e.g., Court Listener, PACER-derived summaries) or
  freelancer-platform-specific dispute statistics (Upwork/Fiverr resolution center data) — those
  could sharpen the freelance-contract findings (scope creep, IP assignment, indemnification) but
  were out of scope given the 12-search/15-fetch budget.
